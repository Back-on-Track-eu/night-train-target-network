explicit ordered list of **real coach types** with per-class sections
(length, weight and crew per section), held as data in `catalog/*.csv`

Composition ids stay synthetic (`<REF|NEW>-<CONCEPT>-<N_COACHES>`);
descriptions anchor to real operations ("similar to"). Crew model:
sum of coach crew factors **plus one Zugchef per train** (1.19 attendant-

### Class cost allocation model (2026-07-22)

Composition costs are allocated to class_mains in two steps, mirroring the
workbook mechanism exactly (verified to 5 decimals against it):

1. **Revenue space** — each class's raw weight = X · (section length /
   composition length excl. service) + (1−X) · (section weight / weight
   excl. service), **normalised to sum to 1** (workbook section weights
   carry a small excl_service quirk for the NEW family, so raw values can
   sum slightly above 1). **X = length_cost_prop, per composition,
   currently 0.7 for all**.
2. **Service areas** (dining car) — their cost fraction (same X-blend of the
   servicing share) is allocated **per place**: every passenger pays equally
   for shared areas regardless of class.

## Price basis policy (2026-07-21)

All monetary parameters follow one of three rules, so every value's year is deliberate rather than accidental:

1. **Recurring labour and lease rates → nominal 2032** (the evaluation year): driver, crew, loco lease — escalated from their source basis at the documented chain (wage chain S19–S21 for labour; 2%/yr general escalation otherwise). **Maintenance and cleaning are the exception since 2026-09-06:** both are set at their observed 2026 level and deliberately *not* escalated — the review showed every observation sitting below the escalated values, and the model's brief is to be optimistic where the evidence allows (see *Operator datapoints*). Stockings stay exempt as before.
2. **Capital → 2026 contract basis, deliberately NOT escalated** (confirmed 2026-09-06: rolling stock for a 2032 network is bought now): rolling stock for a 2032 network is contracted ~2027–2030; amortisation and financing run off the historical contract price, so mid-2020s contract levels are the correct base for a 2032 P&L — escalating them to 2032 would double-count inflation.
3. **Dimensionless parameters** (quotas, periods, availability, dd_premium) → no price basis by construction.

**Benchmark comparability:** the same discipline applies to external figures — every benchmark below is shown with its source price year and normalized to 2032 at 2%/yr, because a 2012-price corridor against 2032 rates is an optical illusion, not a validation:

### Benchmark reconciliation: cost vs annual mileage

The 2026-09-06 review replaced the earlier reconciliation, which had closed a gap that did not exist (7-coach peer against a 14-coach benchmark). Against the corrected peer and the operator datapoints above the picture is: on pure prices our inputs are still *higher* than Ramboll's (coaches €3.39M flat vs their €2.97M mix; staffing priced at S17 wages through a roster-efficiency uplift that Ramboll's 42 €/h all-in attendant rate does not carry); (1) **annual mileage** — their implied ~150–180k km/consist-yr vs our 350k (nightly year-round): every annual fixed cost halves per km at double mileage, worth ~+8–10 €/tkm; (2) **capital accounting** — their 6.5% interest on the undiminished invest + linear AfA ≈ 11.7%/yr of invest vs our amortisation + financing quota ≈ 7.3%/yr (×1.6 on the capital charge — both methods defensible: theirs matches a rental/annuity world, and converges with the French 15-year rental datapoint; ours is ownership economics); (3) **stabling/shunting (Abstellung)** — an explicit S06 cost line our stack carries nowhere: **TO_VERIFY whether calc.py covers stabling anywhere; if not, candidate parameter (~1–2 €/tkm)**.

Evaluated at *their* mileage, our NEW-BAL-14 curve gives 44.8 €/tkm against their ~37.4 operator-only at 2023 prices. **Capital is not where the operator gap sits:** our amortisation + 4% financing quota charges 7.3% of price p.a. for new stock (12.3% refurbished) — a 6.5% annuity gives 7.7% / 12.3%, so the flat quota already *is* an annuity equivalent; Ramboll's interest-on-undiminished-invest method charges ~10%. What remains between us and the ES/RDC level is the coach itself: our refurbished family is a properly refurbished coach (€1.19M, 15 y) whose maintenance-plus-capital stack is ~1.12 €/coach-km at reference mileage, while the KeV all-in usage rate (~1.0 €/coach-km) describes depreciated state-railway fleets — and European Sleeper runs lightly refreshed ex-CNL/ÖBB stock at ~200k km per coach-year, which only works economically because its coach provision is far cheaper than any refit. That is a different product, not a calibration error. If ES confirms an all-in coach cost near the KeV, a third material family ("used, light refit": ~€0.2M acquisition + €0.45M SJ-scope refit ≈ 25 k€/m) is the honest way to reproduce it, and would give the advocacy case three sourced points — cheap today with used stock, better with a proper refit, best with new stock.

![Operator cost vs annual mileage — our curves and all benchmarks](figures/cost_vs_annual_mileage.png)

**Conclusion — route-concrete vs fixed-mileage models (2026-07-20):** the mileage dependency shown above is a *benchmarking artefact of this document*, not a property of the model. Study models (S06, S02, S01) must assume one average annual mileage to produce a single €/train-km figure — and the choice of that average drives their results more than any rate, as the curves demonstrate. **Our evaluation model assumes no mileage at all: it prices each concrete route with its actual length, trip time, and operating pattern**, so utilization is an *output* of the route under evaluation, not an arbitrary input. The fixed 350k km reference in this document exists only to make the calibration comparable to the studies; in production, a route that runs 250 nights instead of 350 automatically carries its true (higher) capital cost per km — which is exactly right, and which no fixed-mileage study model can express. Two consequences: (1) our per-route results are structurally more realistic than any single-average benchmark, and deviations from study figures on individual routes are expected and correct; (2) the utilization lever remains decision-relevant — not as a calibration uncertainty, but as network-design guidance: the model will visibly penalise low-frequency routes, and it should.

### Dienstplanwirkungsgrad (roster efficiency)
<details>
<summary><b>Dienstplanwirkungsgrad</b> — 0.512 driver / 1.000 crew on the reference route <i>(click to expand derivation)</i></summary>

**Value: route-dependent** — reference efficiency 0.60 (driver) and 1.00 (onboard) for a trip inside a single duty, falling to 0.512 / 1.000 on the S41 reference night route (13.5 h driving, 14.5 h on train) — changed 2026-08-11, was a flat 60% / 70% baked into the hourly rate.

**Scope since 2026-09-06: driver only.** Onboard crew are priced per hour on board without a roster division (see *Cabin crew cost*); the parameters below still exist for them in the seed but are set to η_ref = 1.0 with no duty cap.

**Why a variable:** S17 quotes its rates per *deployment hour*, having already divided the raw wage by a Dienstplanwirkungsgrad of 60% (driver) and 70% (onboard). That single number only holds while a trip fits inside one duty. Night trains do not: Directive 2005/47/EC caps **driving time at 8 h on a night shift** (9 h by day), and onboard working time is capped near 24 h by collective agreement, so the reference route is legally **two duties per role**. A relieved crew costs unproductive time — positioning to and from the relief point, a second sign-on/sign-off, and rest away from the home base — which a flat factor cannot express.

**The model:**

> **n_duty = ceil(basis_h / max_duty_h)** — basis: driving time (driver), time on train (onboard)
> **η = η_ref × t_train / (t_train + t_relief × (n_duty − 1))**
> **rate = raw_wage_h / η**

Efficiency therefore forms a sawtooth: it steps down at every duty boundary and recovers within a band as the fixed relief allowance amortises over a longer trip.

| trip (driving / on train) | driver duties | η driver | crew duties | η crew |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6.0 h / 7.0 h | 1 | 0.600 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 8.0 h / 9.0 h | 1 | 0.600 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 9.0 h / 10.0 h | 2 | 0.480 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 11.0 h / 12.0 h | 2 | 0.497 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 13.5 h / 14.5 h | 2 | 0.512 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 16.0 h / 17.0 h | 2 | 0.523 | 1 | 1.000 |
| 17.0 h / 18.0 h | 3 | 0.470 | 1 | 1.000 |

**Derivation, step by step:**

| # | Step | What we did | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Recover the raw wage | Undid S17's own division to get the productive-hour rate behind each deployment-hour figure: driver 40.95, Zugbetreuer 35.90, Zugchef 43.91 EUR/h (2021), then the same wage chain to 2032 as before | 54.16 / 40.00 / 47.60 EUR/h (2032) |
| 2 | Duty caps | Driver: 8 h driving time, the hard legal limit for a night shift (2005/47/EC; night = a period of at least 7 h including 00:00–05:00, so every night train qualifies). Onboard: 24 h working time per collective agreement — not a directive limit, documented assumption | 8 / 24 h |
| 3 | Relief allowance | Each additional duty adds 2.5 h of unproductive paid time (positioning, second sign-on/off, away-base rest handling). No public source prices this; band 1.5–3.0 h, chosen at the middle | 2.5 h per relief |
| 4 | Reference efficiency | Kept S17's 60% / 70% as the *single-duty* value — the interpretation that leaves them untouched wherever a trip needs no relief | 0.60 / 1.00 |
| 5 | Apply to the reference route | 13.5 h driving → 2 driver duties; 14.5 h on train → 2 onboard duties | η 0.512 / 1.000 |
| 6 | Resulting paid rates | raw / η | driver 105.82, attendant 40.0, manager 47.6 EUR/h |
| 7 | Decision (2026-08-11) | Adopted, and moved into the evaluation model rather than the seed: `operator_driver_costs_eur_h` / `operator_crew_costs_eur_h` now carry the raw wage and five new `operators` columns carry the roster parameters, so every route is priced on its own duty structure (CALC 0.9.15) | **variable, seeded as parameters** |

**Honest caveat:** S17's 60% / 70% are SPFV fleet averages that already contain some multi-duty work, so treating them as the single-duty value mildly double-counts — the resulting rates are conservative (biased high). The relief allowance is the free parameter: at 1.5 h the reference rates rise ~10% over the old flat figures, at 3.0 h ~21%; 2.5 h gives ~17%.

**Consequence for network design:** cost per train-km now jumps where a route crosses a duty boundary and eases as it extends within a band. That is a real operating effect the flat factor hid, and it makes duty-boundary-aware timetabling visible in the results.

The relief allowance and the onboard duty cap are documented assumptions — no source prices either.
