# Energy model calibration

Traction energy consumption for a passenger night train, calibrated against
Deutsche Bahn's Trassenfinder API across the German network.

`sources/` is the calibration record and `seed/` is what the database reads.
`data/` sits between them: written by `01` and `01b`, read by `02`.

**Everything that talks to the API lives in `trassenfinder.py`.** Both

search setting in the module, not in a notebook.

## The model (final, v1.1.0 — 2026-08-30)

```
E = a·m + d·( b + c·m + (k_m·m + k_L·L)·v̄² )  +  (P_loco + P_hotel·n) · t
    \__________________ traction __________________/   \______ aux ______/
```

per country leg, where `m` is coach mass in tonnes (80% load, locomotive
excluded — Trassenfinder's Wagenzugmasse basis), `L` coach rake length in
metres, `n` the coach count, `d` leg distance in km, `v̄` the realised
average speed in km/h and `t` the running time in hours. Shipped values:
`models/energy/calibrated_coefficients.py`, REGENERATED by `02` — the
table below documents the estimation basis, the module holds the numbers.

Drag is the combined nonnegative form `k_m·m + k_L·L`: the mass-matched
pairs (+17% length at equal mass → +6–8% drag) rule out pure-mass, and the
cross-group fit rules out pure-length carrying it alone. The per-leg
constant `a` is clamped to ≥ 0 — it carries start/stop energy, and without
it legs under 50 km under-predict by roughly a third. `P_hotel` (15
kW/coach) is an ASSUMPTION, not a measurement: the collection ran with
coach hotel load off (energy_wagen_kwh = 0 throughout), installed comfort
power is 50–100 kVA per coach and the UIC train-line cap is 50 kVA. It is
linear in time, so revising it needs no re-collection.

The drag coefficient is identified over realised average speeds of 56–166
km/h. Above that the v² term extrapolates — deliberately unclamped,
because the quadratic exponent is verified on the sweep
(`exponent_check.py`) and Trassenfinder's own ~196/~220 km/h running-time
ceiling is a tool property, not physics: real 230 km/h stock runs 230
where the line allows.

The two parts are fitted separately because they respond to different things.
Traction scales with the square of speed; auxiliaries draw power for as long as
the train is moving, so they grow as it slows down. Collected as one total —
which is all `01` recorded before 2026-08-30 — a single speed coefficient has to
absorb both and comes out wrong at the slow end.

| symbol | column | unit | estimated from |
|---|---|---|---|
| `a` | `energy_factor_base` | kWh/t | `samples_all` |
| `b` | `energy_factor_distance` | kWh/km | `samples_all` |
| `c` | `energy_factor_weight` | kWh/(t·km) | `samples_all` |
| `k` | `energy_factor_speed` | kWh/(t·km·(km/h)²) | `samples_speed`, mass form |
| `k1` | `energy_factor_speed_base` | kWh/(km·(km/h)²) | `samples_speed`, length form |
| `k2` | `energy_factor_speed_length` | kWh/(m·km·(km/h)²) | `samples_speed`, length form |
| `P_aux` | `energy_aux_power_kw` | kW | `samples_all`, energy split |
| — | `energy_factor_terrain` | kWh/(t·km) per point | not estimable, held at 0 |

No intercept and no centring constants: every term is proportional to something
physical, so every coefficient has a home in the schema and a unit that means
something.

### Mass basis — the thing that will bite you

`weight_t` in every sample is the **Wagenzugmasse**: coaches at 80% load,
**excluding** the 87 t locomotive, because that is what `01` sends as
`wagenzugmasse_t` while the locomotive is specified separately by
`triebfahrzeug.hauptnummer`.

The backend has both quantities. `Composition.total_weight_t()` is coaches only
and is the one to use. `total_gross_weight_t()` adds the locomotives, and
applying these coefficients to it counts the locomotive twice — the locomotive's
own contribution is already inside `b`. That is roughly +11% on a 500 km leg.
`02` asserts the basis rather than trusting the column name.

## Does drag scale with mass, or with length?

The model form ties the drag term to **mass**, and the physics does not.
Aerodynamic drag comes from frontal area and from skin friction along the
train's length; mass belongs in rolling resistance and acceleration.

Across the refurbished fleet the two are indistinguishable, because mass and
length move together at 1.98–2.11 t/m. The new-build compositions sit at
1.69 t/m and break that. The 2026-08-30 collection shows the difference at
constant mass:

| pair | mass | length | energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| REF-COUCH-6 → NEW-BAL-7 | +0.26% | +17.2% | +5.2% |
| REF-BUD-12 → NEW-BAL-14 | −1.32% | +17.2% | +3.4% |

`NEW-BAL-14` is *lighter* than `REF-BUD-12` and uses more energy. A drag
coefficient fitted only on refurbished stock and applied to new-build stock is
therefore biased, on exactly the refurbished-versus-new comparison the tool
exists to make.

So the sweep runs a **factorial design over mass and length**: two mass-matched
pairs, 17% apart in length, plus a mid-range anchor. `02` estimates a drag slope
per group in stage one, then regresses it across groups on mass and on length —
the comparison a within-group estimator cannot make.

**The mass-matched pairs decide which form is adopted, not R².** Drag per km
tracks train size under either form, so both fit well and the margin between
them is noise: on a length-truth test the mass form still reached R² 0.991
against the length form's 0.995. The pairs are unambiguous — under the mass
form they must show no gap at all, since the mass is the same. `k2` also comes
out significant under mass truth, purely from collinearity, so significance
alone cannot decide it either. `DRAG_FORM = "auto"` requires both a pair gap
over 5% and a significant `k2`; `"mass"` or `"length"` force one.

## Why speed is estimated separately

`01` sends the composition's own `v_max`, 200 or 230 km/h. Both sit above line
speed on essentially every path, so the booked speed never binds:

- the 230 km/h compositions run **0.08 min slower** on average than the 200 km/h
  ones on the same segment; median difference is exactly 0
- between-segment SD of average speed is **11.94 km/h**, within-segment SD
  **1.10 km/h** — about 99% of the variation is a property of the line
- what within-segment variation exists is mass: median correlation between mass
  and speed inside a segment is **−0.92**

Fit a speed term on that and you get a negative coefficient (−4.14 kWh per km/h,
p < 0.0001). It is real in the data and meaningless as physics: it reads "slow
route" as "slow train", and slow routes are branch lines and station approaches,
which cost more per tonne-km for reasons unrelated to drag. Shipping it would
mean a faster composition predicted to use less energy.

`01b` breaks the confound by fixing the segment and the composition and varying
only the booked `v_max`, **downward** from 200 km/h. `02` then estimates `k`
within `(segment, composition)` groups and holds it fixed while refitting the
level terms on `samples_all`.

## Regenerating

**Normal case — the samples already exist.** Run `02` only. `ensure_local()`
pulls `samples_*.csv` from Drive if this machine has never collected them.

**After changing route lists, compositions or request settings.** Run `01`, then
upload the new `data/samples_*.csv` to the Drive folder, then run `02`. `01` is
not idempotent against the API: Trassenfinder's network state moves between
infrastructure versions, so two runs months apart will not agree exactly.

**To re-estimate the speed coefficient.** Run `01b`, upload
`data/samples_speed.csv`, run `02`. `01b` is about 430 requests, a quarter of
what `01` costs. It anchors itself against `samples_all` at `v_max=200` and
warns if the two no longer agree.

Everything under `data/` and `seed/` is generated — **never hand-edit it**, the
next run overwrites the change silently. To change a value, change the notebook.

### Where this deviates from the other calib packages

Everywhere else, `db/dev/seed.py` regenerates a domain's seed CSVs by running
both notebooks when they are absent. **Here it can only run `02`.** `01` makes
roughly 1,560 HTTP calls to an external API over 30–50 minutes; that cannot
happen at container start, and a machine with no network to Trassenfinder could
never seed at all.

So the collected samples travel through Drive instead of being rebuilt.
`data_sources.ensure_local()` fetches them on first use, mirroring the ONTD seed
pattern — a local file always wins, and the sync only fills gaps. Put the folder
id in `backend/docker/.env`:

```
ENERGY_DRIVE_FOLDER_ID=<folder id>
```

Without it, `02` fails with a message pointing at `01`. That is the intended
degradation: no silent fallback to a stale or partial sample.

Three helpers, deliberately different:

- `ensure_local(name)` — comes from outside this machine. Downloaded when absent.
- `local_input(name, produced_by)` — written by an earlier step here. Never
  downloaded, because a stale Drive copy could override what `01` just wrote.
- `source_input(name)` — a committed input under `sources/`.

## Poolability of the two route sources

The 2026-08-30 run puts a source indicator on the per-km term at
p = 0.0317, +0.46 kWh/km. That is marginal, localized to the synthetic
long-route set, and ≈4% of intensity there; pooling is retained and the
effect documented rather than modelled. If a future collection widens it,
split the per-km constant by source before anything else.

## The route sources

| Source | Routes | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| `ontd` | 95 | Real German night-train segments from the ONTD workbook |
| `synthetic` | 100 | Generated station pairs, sampled for geographic and length diversity |
| `speed` | 15 + 8 | A distance-stratified subset plus eight deliberately chosen high-speed corridors, re-queried across the booked-speed grid in two strata |

ONTD segments are short by construction — real night trains stop often, median
78 km — which leaves the long-distance range thin. The generated pairs extend
it and act as an out-of-sample check on whether the fit describes moving a
train or how night trains happen to be routed. They are kept in separate files
with a `source` column so pooling stays a deliberate choice.

### What the 2026-08-24 run showed

The two samples agree. Fitting `E = a·m + d·(b + c·m)` separately:

| sample | samples | routes | `a` | `b` | `c` | R² |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ontd | 760 | 95 | 0.0673 | 2.0314 | 0.007309 | 0.986 |
| synthetic | 424 | 53 | 0.1066 | 2.0612 | 0.007586 | 0.993 |
| pooled | 1,184 | 148 | 0.0453 | 2.0547 | 0.007621 | 0.991 |

The per-km terms agree within 1.5% and 3.8%, a source indicator on the distance
term is not significant (p = 0.21), and fleet-weighted kWh/km per composition
matches within 1% for all eight. Cross-prediction costs −3.5% bias one way and
+5.1% the other. **The samples are poolable**, and pooling is what supplies the
400–941 km range where ONTD has only 80 samples and no coverage past 817 km.

Those coefficients carry no speed term. Once `k` is estimated from a sweep, `b`
and `c` must be refitted with the speed offset subtracted — they will move,
because they currently absorb the average aerodynamic contribution over the
80–110 km/h band the sample covers.

### Why 47 of the 100 generated routes fail

Worth understanding before anyone tries to "fix" it.

| kind | routes |
|---|---|
| `Es konnte keine Route … gefunden werden` | 38 |
| `Ungültige Betriebsstelle` (dropped in the pre-flight) | 7 |
| `kein zulässiger Halteplatz` (Oppendorf) | 2 |

All eight compositions fail identically on every failed route, which rules out
anything train-dependent — mass, length, `v_max` — and puts the cause in the
fixed request settings or the network itself.

The routing failures track station category sharply: 22% for long-distance to
long-distance, 58% mixed, 71% regional to regional. A `D4` locomotive-hauled
train frequently cannot reach a branch-line station under these settings, and
`streckenklasse: "D4"` with `knotenbahnhoefe_meiden: True` are the likely
constraints. `D4` is over-specified for a night train — roughly 1.9 t/m against
the 8.0 t/m the class demands — so relaxing it would open lower-class lines.

**That relaxation has not been made, deliberately.** A lower line class lets the
40/30/30 optimiser pick different routes, including on ONTD segments that route
fine today, so it would invalidate the existing samples and require a full
re-run. There is also a reading in which the failures are correct: these pairs
are not usable by a locomotive-hauled night train, which is what the tool
models. The consequence to carry is that the 53 surviving generated routes are
biased towards main-line pairs — a selection, not a random draw.

## Three things the collection gets wrong if you are not careful

**`mutter` is a property of the station, not a request option.** Trassenfinder
rejects a Mutterbetriebsstelle sent as a child and a child sent as a mother,
both with HTTP 400 and no indication which end is at fault. Hard-coding
`mutter: True` cost 40 of 96 ONTD segments, biased towards short ones. `01`
resolves the flag once per station in a pre-flight pass before any collection
starts, so an invalid DS100 costs one request instead of eight.

**Response bodies must survive the failure path.** `raise_for_status()` inside a
bare `except` discards the API's message, which is the only thing that says
which station was rejected. `TrassenfinderError` carries it.

**An unresolvable station is dropped, not warned about.** With no `mutter` flag
its requests fall back to the default and return HTTP 400 — eight wasted calls
per route, and a failure table in which the genuine routing failures are buried.

## Two things the sweep gets wrong if you are not careful

**The route is not guaranteed to stay fixed.** `gewichtung_parameter` puts 30%
of the route-choice weight on energy, so a lower `v_max` can make a different
path optimal. A group whose `distance_km` moves across the grid is no longer a
controlled experiment. `01b` §6 flags them and `02` uses only the stable ones.
The fallback is a distance-only weighting, which pins the route harder but makes
the sweep incomparable with `samples_all`.

**A sample's ceiling is not the network's ceiling.** The first extended sweep
topped out at 130 km/h realised and booked 230 gained nothing, which read as a
network limit. It was a property of 21 segments drawn by distance band: only
five rerouted onto high-speed line at all, and those were partial NBS runs.
Reading "absent from this sample" as "does not occur" is the same error as the
bremshundertstel one — a limit of the data taken for a property of the world.

The German network carries 250-300 km/h line and the target network is
European, where LGVs run 300-320. So `NBS_CORRIDORS` in `01b` §1 names eight
corridors chosen *because* they are high-speed, appended to the sampled draw
rather than left to chance, and `PER_BAND` was trimmed to pay for them.

**Average speed is not a speed profile.** Drag is convex in speed, so a leg
averaging 130 km/h with a 230 km/h section and slow approaches burns more than
one held steady at 130. The model fits and predicts on the average, which holds
only while the profile shape resembles the calibration sample's. That is the
second reason the high-speed corridors are in the sweep.

**Sweeping above the line speed is a no-op.** Line speed binds. The grid has to go down from
200 km/h, and `01b` §7 reports realised average speed per grid point so you can
see how much of the grid actually bound.

**Not every distance band is worth the same.** On a short leg the train never
reaches 200 km/h anyway, so lowering the cap changes little and those groups
contribute almost no variation in realised speed. `PER_BAND` in `01b` §2 is
therefore weighted towards long segments.

**Measure binding per grid point, not per band.** The 2026-08-29 run showed why:
realised speed gained 17.2 km/h from booked 80 to 100, 4.0 from 100 to 120, and
exactly 0.0 at every point above — 140, 160 and 200 returned byte-identical
results, so half the grid bought nothing. A span measured across the whole grid
looked healthy in every distance band (19-27 km/h) and hid it completely,
because all the movement was in the bottom third. `01b` §7 now prints the gap
each grid point gains over the one below, and the count of distinct realised
speeds per group.

**High-speed lines are a stratum, not a grid point.** With
`schnellfahrstrecken_meiden: True` the conventional network caps the train
around 120 km/h booked, so 120-200 is unreachable. Allowing high-speed lines
makes it bind — but it changes which *line* the train runs on, so toggling it
inside a group would put a route change back into the speed response, which is
the confound the sweep exists to break. It is therefore held constant within a
group and the group key is `(segment, composition, stratum)`.

Night trains do not in practice use high-speed lines (night-time maintenance
windows), which does not matter here: the stratum identifies the *train's* drag
coefficient, not its route, and drag does not care which line it is on. The
steeper NBS gradients are constant within a group and the fixed effect absorbs
them. What it buys is a second, independent estimate of `k` over a speed range
the conventional network cannot reach. `02` §3 reports both and compares them.

One caveat to carry: NBS are tunnel-heavy and tunnel drag is materially higher
than open-air drag. An `sfs` estimate above the conventional one has that
mundane explanation before it has an interesting one.

## Braking and line class are derived, not fixed

Both were wrong in the template until 2026-08-30 and both bounded what the whole
collection could say. `trassenfinder.py` now derives them per composition.

### Bremshundertstel

`BrH = 100 × Σ braked weight / train mass`, and it sets the permitted speed
through the brake tables. The template carried **70**, a freight-grade value.
Measured on Hamburg–Hannover: 70 BrH gives 110.3 km/h average, 130 gives 157.3,
and energy rises 81% between them.

Braked weight is not published per vehicle for this fleet, so
`bremshundertstel()` models it as a ratio of vehicle mass, anchored on two
references: a disc-braked coach in R without Mg (31 t mass, 55 t braked → 1.77)
and a modern IC coach in R+Mg (about 111 t braked on roughly 51 t → about 2.15,
with a whole IC reaching about 205 BrH). `COACH_BRAKED_WEIGHT_RATIO` is 2.10 and
`LOCO_BRAKED_WEIGHT_T` is 120.

That yields **194–201 BrH** across the eight standard compositions. The spread
is real: the locomotive's braked weight relative to its own mass is lower than a
coach's, so short formations come out a few points below long ones. Both DB
deduction rules are checked rather than assumed — every composition clears the
170 threshold for the R+Mg 8 t deduction, and the longest Wagenzug at 371.4 m is
under the 400 m length deduction.

Mg is mandatory above 160 km/h and has no representation in Trassenfinder's
`bremsstellung` field, so its contribution is carried in the braked weight,
which is where it belongs in a real brake calculation anyway.

### Bremsstellung

**R.** Vehicles cleared for more than 120 km/h carry R on the brake changeover,
and P is the 120 km/h position. Note that P and R returned identical results at
70 BrH — not because the position is irrelevant, but because the brake
percentage binds first and the position never gets a chance to matter.

### Streckenklasse

**D2**, derived by `streckenklasse()` from EN 15528 rather than assumed.

The letter is set by the locomotive: a Vectron at 87 t on four axles is
**21.75 t per axle**, which needs D (22.5 t) and rules out every C class
(20.0 t) whatever the metre load. The digit is set by the heaviest vehicle per
metre — again the locomotive, at **4.58 t/m** — which clears the lowest digit
(6.4 t/m) comfortably.

So D4 was over-specified **only in its digit**. The earlier note in the
decisions log reasoned from the Wagenzug's 1.9–2.1 t/m and concluded the whole
class was too high; that missed the locomotive's axle load. Relaxing D4 → D2 is
correct and opens D2 and D3 lines, but **it will not rescue the branch-line
routing failures**: those lines are typically C class or below, and the
locomotive cannot use them at all. D4 covers 28,577 km, about 86% of the DB
InfraGO network, with the remaining 4,708 km mostly Neben- and Stadtschnellbahnen.

## The per-point energy fields are cumulative

`routenpunkte[i].energieverbrauch_info` carries **running totals along the
route**, not per-segment increments. Point 0 reads zero and the last point
carries the whole trip, so the value wanted is `routenpunkte[-1]`, never the sum.

This is worth stating loudly because summing looked entirely reasonable and
failed silently. The first collection to use the split summed them and produced
an auxiliary share of **606% of total energy**, with the components running 22x
the trip total at the median and 145x on the longest route. No exception, no
warning, and every other column in the file was correct.

The signature, if it ever recurs: the overshoot scales with the number of route
points, and a two-point route comes out exactly right. `01`'s quality check now
reconciles the split automatically, and `02` refuses to fit a sample whose
components sum to more than 1.05x the total.

`energy_components_kwh` carries the components' own reported total alongside
`energy_kwh` from `zusammenfassung`, rather than assuming they agree. If
regeneration is netted off one and not the other, that shows up as a column to
inspect instead of disappearing into the fit.

## Validate the payload before trusting a collection

`00_payload_validation.ipynb` exists because a request field that is constant
across every row is invisible in the data and not therefore harmless. Constants
set the level, and a constant that caps speed silently bounds what the whole
dataset can ever say.

The case that prompted it: the 2026-08-30 sweep found realised speed pinned at
97 km/h with every booked value above 120 returning byte-identical results, and
allowing high-speed lines moved it by 3.2 km/h while genuinely rerouting the
train onto shorter paths. The template carries `bremsstellung: "P"`, valid to
120 km/h, and `bremshundertstel: 70`, a freight-grade braked-weight percentage.
Its other defaults — `wagenzugmasse_t: 1200`, `hauptnummer: "6185"` — say it was
adapted from a freight example by changing `verkehrsart` and little else.

Run `00` whenever `PAYLOAD_TEMPLATE` changes, and whenever a collected number
looks wrong. It dumps a full unreduced response, tests the brake fields
directly, and prints technical running times on corridors whose real timings a
reader will recognise.

## Open

Seven S-Bahn and local stops in `sources/routes_synthetic.csv` are not
Betriebsstellen — `MURB`, `RBSS`, `BSAL`, `ADT`, `AOH`, `BWSS`, `AKWS`. The
pre-flight drops them every run; removing them from the route list would save
the probe requests.

No terrain coefficient is estimable from any source here: all are Germany-only,
so `terrain_score` has no variance. Austrian and Swiss routes are a separate
collection — a new route list, not a re-run.

Regenerative braking is not separable. `rueckspeisung_euro_pro_kwh` is set in
the request but the returned figure is not decomposed, so recovery sits inside
the fitted constants at whatever rate Trassenfinder assumes.
